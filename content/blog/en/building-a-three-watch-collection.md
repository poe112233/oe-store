Most collections grow by accident. Someone buys what catches their eye, then another, and three years later there are nine watches and two that get worn. A deliberate three-watch collection is more useful than an accidental ten.

## The framework

Three watches, each covering a different need:

1. **The everyday** — worn 60–70% of the time.
2. **The sporty one** — worn when the everyday is wrong.
3. **The dress one** — worn a handful of times a year, and that is fine.

The goal is not variety for its own sake. It is that any situation you realistically encounter is covered, and none of the three sits in a drawer.

## Slot 1: the everyday

This is the watch you will wear most, so it must be genuinely comfortable and slightly boring.

**What to prioritise:**
- **Versatility** — works with a shirt and with a t-shirt.
- **Size** — 36–40 mm, lug-to-lug under 48 mm for most wrists.
- **Water resistance** — 100 m minimum, so rain and handwashing are non-issues.
- **Legibility** — you will read it a hundred times a day.
- **Serviceability** — a common movement means parts and watchmakers are easy to find.

**What to avoid:** anything with a strong personality. Bold colours, oversized cases and unusual shapes are the ones that end up unworn.

A steel case, neutral dial, and a bracelet or versatile strap covers more situations than anything else.

## Slot 2: the sporty one

The watch for weekends, travel, and anything active.

**What to prioritise:**
- **Robustness** — a bezel you can actually use, or a case that shrugs off knocks.
- **Water resistance** — 200 m or better if you swim.
- **Contrast** — legible at a glance, in bad light.
- **A different feel from the everyday** — otherwise you will not reach for it.

**What to avoid:** buying a second everyday watch with a different dial colour. That is one slot filled twice.

## Slot 3: the dress one

Worn rarely, and should feel special when it is.

**What to prioritise:**
- **Thinness** — under 10 mm, so it sits under a cuff.
- **Simplicity** — a clean dial ages better than a busy one.
- **A strap you can change** — leather in winter, something lighter in summer.
- **Restraint** — this is the watch that should not announce itself.

**What to avoid:** spending the most on the least-worn watch. It is a common mistake and it is expensive.

## The mistakes that break collections

**Buying the same watch three times.** Three dive watches with different bezels is one watch, three times. Each slot should be a genuinely different tool.

**Letting budget concentrate in the wrong place.** Most people should spend most on the everyday watch, because it gets the most wear. Enthusiasts often do the opposite.

**Buying for a life you do not have.** A 300 m dive watch is lovely; if you never swim, a 100 m everyday watch serves you better.

**Ignoring service costs.** A watch with an unusual movement may need to go back to the manufacturer for servicing, which is slow and expensive. Three watches from mainstream calibres will cost less to own over ten years than one exotic piece.

**Buying all three at once.** Wear the first one for six months. It will tell you what the second one should be — and it is usually not what you expected.

## A note on straps

The cheapest way to get more range out of three watches is straps, not watches.

- **Steel bracelet** — most versatile, most durable.
- **Leather** — dresses a watch up instantly.
- **Rubber / FKM** — genuinely waterproof, good for sport.
- **Textile / NATO** — casual, cheap, easy to swap.

Three watches with two straps each is effectively six looks, for the price of three straps.

## When to stop at three

When each one gets worn, none of them needs servicing at the same time, and you do not have a mental list of gaps. That is the signal.

If you find yourself wanting a fourth, ask what specific situation it covers that the first three do not. If the answer is "none, I just like it" — that is fine too, but call it what it is: a collection, not a toolkit.
