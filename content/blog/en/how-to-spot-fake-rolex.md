A Rolex is one of the most counterfeited objects on earth, and the copies have gotten good. Modern fakes reproduce case dimensions and dial layout closely enough that a glance at arm's length tells you nothing. What still separates them is the small physical details — the ones that cost money to get right.

This is not a guide to authenticating a watch you are about to spend five figures on. For that, you want a watchmaker with a loupe and a caseback opener. This is a guide to the details worth checking when you are comparing examples, so you know what "well made" actually looks like.

## 1. Dial printing

Genuine dial text is applied with a pad-printing process that lays down crisp, slightly raised ink. Under 10× magnification the edges are sharp, the letter spacing is even, and the print sits perfectly centred in its allotted space.

Common tells on a weak copy:

- Letters that look slightly fuzzy or bleed at the edges.
- Inconsistent spacing, especially around the word "OYSTER" or the depth rating line.
- Text that sits too high or too low relative to the minute track.

The line at the bottom of the dial — "SWISS MADE" on modern references — should be perfectly centred with the minute markers on either side.

## 2. Hands and markers

Look at the edges of the hands. On a well-finished watch they are bevelled and polished, so they catch light along a clean line. Cheap copies often have flat, stamped hands with rounded or burred edges.

Luminous material is the other tell. Applied lume should sit level inside its frame, not overflow the edges. Under UV, a modern genuine example glows a consistent blue; older tritium dials will not glow at all. A fake that glows bright green under UV is almost certainly using generic paint.

## 3. Bezel action and alignment

On a dive model, rotate the bezel. It should move in crisp, evenly spaced clicks — 60 of them on a Submariner — with no wobble and no mushiness between positions. The triangle at 12 should line up exactly with the dial's 12 o'clock marker.

On ceramic bezels, the engraved numerals should look filled and slightly recessed, not printed on the surface. Run a fingernail across them: genuine ceramic inserts have a distinct depth.

## 4. Crown and tube

Unscrew the crown. The threads should feel smooth and precise, not gritty. On the winding position, turning the crown should feel like winding a fine mechanism — light resistance, no grinding.

The coronet stamped on the crown's end is a small but useful check: it should be crisp, correctly proportioned, and centred.

## 5. Bracelet and clasp

The clasp is where cost-cutting shows up most reliably. Open it and look inside. Genuine clasps have deeply stamped or engraved markings, a properly finished inner surface, and a spring bar that snaps with authority.

Check the bracelet's end links: they should fit the case lugs with almost no gap, and the articulation should be smooth without rattling.

## 6. Movement

If the caseback is transparent, you get the most information. A genuine automatic movement shows:

- Consistent finishing across bridges — Geneva stripes, not printed patterns.
- A rotor that spins freely and quietly.
- A balance wheel oscillating at a steady rate (28,800 vph on most modern Rolex calibres).

If you can hear a loud, coarse tick from across the room, you are not looking at a mechanical Rolex.

## 7. Papers, serial and consistency

The warranty card, the serial between the lugs, and the reference number all need to agree. A mismatch is a red flag regardless of how good the watch looks. Check that the card's model matches the case, and that the serial is engraved — not laser-etched on top of the surface.

## The honest summary

None of these checks require expertise, but each one requires attention. A watch that passes all seven is very likely correct. A watch that fails two or three is very likely not, no matter how convincing it feels in the hand.
