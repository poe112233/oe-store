Cross-border shipping has three separate stages, and confusion usually comes from treating them as one: the seller's processing time, the transit time, and customs clearance. They behave differently and fail for different reasons.

## The three stages

**Processing (1–5 days).** Picking, packing, and handing to the carrier. Slow here usually means the item is being sourced.

**Transit (7–20 days typical).** Depends entirely on the route and the service level chosen. Economy lines are cheap and slow; express lines are faster and cost more.

**Customs clearance (0–14 days).** The least predictable stage. Most parcels clear in hours. A minority get held, and the reason is almost always documentation or a value threshold.

## The threshold question

Every destination has a *de minimis* value — below it, no duty is charged.

| Region | Typical de minimis |
|---|---|
| United States | USD 800 |
| European Union | EUR 150 (VAT applies from EUR 0) |
| United Kingdom | GBP 135 |
| Canada | CAD 150 |
| Australia | AUD 1,000 |

Two things trip people up here:

1. **VAT and duty are different.** In the EU, VAT applies from the first euro; the EUR 150 threshold is for *duty*, not VAT. Many buyers assume "under 150 means nothing to pay" and are surprised by a VAT invoice.
2. **Thresholds change.** These figures are current as of 2026 but they are policy, not physics. Check before ordering if the amount matters to you.

## The four common causes of delay

**Incomplete address.** Missing apartment number, wrong postcode, or a name that does not match the recipient. This is the single most common cause.

**Restricted items.** Batteries, liquids, magnets, branded goods in some jurisdictions. If a parcel is stopped for this, it usually goes back rather than forward.

**Under-declared value.** Declaring far below the actual value can trigger inspection, and the outcome is worse than paying the duty.

**Peak season.** Late November through December, and around major sales events, transit times extend by roughly 30–50%. Nothing is wrong; the network is simply full.

## What to do when a parcel is held

You will usually get a notice asking for one of three things:

- **Payment of duty/VAT** — pay it, keep the receipt.
- **Proof of value** — an invoice or payment screenshot.
- **Recipient confirmation** — ID or a signed form.

Respond quickly. Held parcels often have a storage window (commonly 5–14 days) after which they are returned.

## Packaging expectations

A well-packed parcel has:

- The item in a dust bag or protective sleeve.
- Void fill so the item cannot move — listen for rattling when you pick it up.
- Sealed outer box with no external branding.
- Documentation inside, not taped to the outside.

If a box arrives with crushed corners and the item is fine, that is normal. If you can hear the item moving, photograph it before opening — that is your evidence if there is damage.

## Practical timeline planning

If you need something by a specific date:

| Occasion | Order by |
|---|---|
| Standard delivery | 3 weeks before |
| Holiday season (Nov–Dec) | 5–6 weeks before |
| Event with fixed date | 6 weeks before, express line |

Add a week if your address is rural or if the destination country has a reputation for slow clearance.

## The honest summary

Most orders arrive without incident. The ones that go wrong go wrong for boring reasons: a missing apartment number, a value threshold nobody checked, or a hold notice that sat unread for a week.

Track the parcel, read the notices, and keep the paperwork. That covers the overwhelming majority of what can go wrong between checkout and your door.
