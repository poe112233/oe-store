The movement is the engine, and it determines almost everything about how a watch behaves: how often you have to touch it, how accurate it is, how much it costs to service, and how long it will last.

There are four types worth understanding.

## Manual wind

You turn the crown to wind a mainspring, which releases energy through a gear train to the escapement.

**Pros:** thin movements, no rotor to obscure the mechanism, a daily ritual some people genuinely enjoy.

**Cons:** you must wind it, typically every 38–48 hours. Forget and it stops.

**Suits:** dress watches, collectors, anyone who wears the same watch every day.

## Automatic

Same mechanism, but a rotor on the back winds the mainspring as your arm moves. No winding required as long as you wear it.

**Pros:** wear-and-forget, no battery, generally the best all-round choice.

**Cons:** thicker than manual (the rotor needs space), needs a minimum of movement — a watch left on a desk will stop within 40–70 hours.

**Suits:** almost everyone, for daily wear.

**Power reserve** is the number that matters here: 38–42 hours is standard, 70 hours is excellent, 120+ hours means you can take it off for the weekend and it will still be running on Monday.

## Quartz

A battery drives a quartz crystal, which oscillates at a precise frequency and drives the hands via a stepping motor.

**Pros:** extremely accurate (typically ±15 seconds per *month*), cheap, thin, low maintenance.

**Cons:** battery replacement every 2–3 years, a "ticking" second hand some find less characterful, and a lower ceiling on craftsmanship.

**Suits:** anyone who wants accuracy and zero fuss, tool watches, everyday beaters.

## Spring drive / hybrid

A few manufacturers combine a mainspring with quartz regulation — the spring provides power, a quartz oscillator controls the rate. The result is mechanical feel with quartz-like accuracy, and a famously smooth sweeping second hand.

**Pros:** the accuracy of quartz with the character of mechanical.

**Cons:** expensive, made by very few companies, service network is limited.

**Suits:** enthusiasts who want the best of both and are willing to pay for it.

## Comparing on the numbers

| | Manual | Automatic | Quartz |
|---|---|---|---|
| Accuracy (daily) | ±5–15 s | ±2–20 s | ±0.5 s |
| Power source | Mainspring | Mainspring (self-winding) | Battery |
| Service interval | 5–7 years | 5–10 years | Battery 2–3 yrs, service rare |
| Typical thickness | Thinnest | Thickest | Thin |
| Cost to service | Moderate | Highest | Lowest |

## What the specs actually mean for you

**If you wear a watch daily:** automatic. It winds itself, it is accurate enough, and it will last decades with servicing.

**If you wear one occasionally:** quartz. An automatic left in a drawer will stop; a quartz will still be right when you pick it up. Alternatively, an automatic with a 70-hour power reserve and a winder.

**If you care about thinness:** manual or quartz. The rotor in an automatic adds 1–2 mm.

**If you care about accuracy above all:** quartz or spring drive. Mechanical accuracy is impressive for what it is, but it is not close.

**If this is your first serious watch:** automatic, with a 40-hour-plus reserve. It is the type most people end up keeping.

## Two myths worth retiring

**"Quartz is cheap."** Cheap quartz exists, but so do high-end quartz movements (thermocompensated, ±5 seconds per *year*). The type tells you less than the execution.

**"Automatic is more accurate than quartz."** No. Automatic is more *interesting*. Quartz wins on accuracy by an order of magnitude and always has.

## A note on servicing

Mechanical movements need cleaning and re-lubrication every 5–10 years, and that costs real money — often 5–10% of the watch's value. Factor it in. A $500 automatic that needs a $150 service every seven years is still a good deal; a $2,000 one that needs a $400 service is a different calculation.

Quartz movements are far more forgiving, largely because there are fewer moving parts to wear.
