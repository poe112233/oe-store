Almost every sizing question starts with case diameter, and almost every sizing mistake comes from trusting it. A 40 mm watch from one brand can wear like a 42 mm from another, and a 38 mm can look smaller than a 36 mm depending on the lugs.

Here is what actually determines how a watch sits on your wrist.

## The three numbers that matter

**Case diameter** — the width across the case, excluding the crown. This is what brands advertise, and it is the least informative of the three.

**Lug-to-lug** — the distance from the tip of the top lugs to the tip of the bottom lugs. This is the number that decides whether a watch overhangs your wrist. It varies by up to 8 mm between watches of identical diameter.

**Thickness** — how tall the case sits. This decides whether the watch slides under a shirt cuff and how top-heavy it feels.

## How to measure your wrist

Wrap a strip of paper or a soft tape around your wrist just below the wrist bone, snug but not tight. Note the length in millimetres.

If you have no tape, use a piece of string and measure it against a ruler.

**Rough guidance by wrist circumference:**

| Wrist | Comfortable lug-to-lug | Typical diameter |
|---|---|---|
| 150–160 mm | up to 45 mm | 34–38 mm |
| 160–175 mm | up to 48 mm | 36–40 mm |
| 175–190 mm | up to 52 mm | 40–42 mm |
| 190 mm+ | up to 55 mm | 42–44 mm |

The lug-to-lug column is the one to use. As a rule, the lugs should sit within the flat top of your wrist, not hang over the edges.

## Why 36 mm came back

Through the 2000s the market pushed towards larger cases — 42, 44, sometimes 46 mm. The correction started around 2018 and has been steady since.

The reason is simple: a smaller case with a well-proportioned dial reads as more refined on most wrists, and it fits under a cuff. A 36 mm watch with a thin bezel can have as much visible dial as a 40 mm with a thick one.

If you have never worn a 36 mm and assume it will look tiny, try one. Most people are surprised.

## Proportion, not size

Two watches of the same diameter can look completely different depending on:

- **Bezel thickness** — a thin bezel makes the dial look larger and the watch look bigger.
- **Dial colour** — dark dials read smaller, light dials read larger.
- **Lug shape** — straight lugs look longer; downturned lugs hug the wrist.
- **Bracelet taper** — a bracelet that tapers sharply makes the case look larger by contrast.

This is why comparing by diameter alone fails. Look at photos of the watch on a wrist, or better, look at the lug-to-lug figure.

## Thickness: the overlooked dimension

A 12 mm watch and a 14 mm watch feel like different categories.

- Under 10 mm: dress watch territory, slides under any cuff.
- 10–12 mm: versatile, works with a shirt.
- 12–14 mm: sporty, may catch on a tight cuff.
- 14 mm+: chunky by design, needs a looser sleeve.

If you wear tailored clothing regularly, thickness matters more than diameter.

## A practical method

1. Measure your wrist.
2. Find the lug-to-lug of the watch you are considering — not the diameter.
3. Compare that figure to your wrist comfort range from the table above.
4. Check the thickness against what you normally wear.
5. Only then look at photos.

Doing it in this order avoids the most common mistake: falling for a photo of a watch on a 190 mm wrist and discovering it swallows your 160 mm one.
